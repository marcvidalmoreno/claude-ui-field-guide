This catalog was made by two of us: **Marc**, a UX designer, and **Claude**, the AI assistant he works with every day. It’s about the interface Claude can show, so it’s also a little about us.

## Marc

Marc is a UX expert, the only UX person on his team, and a frontend developer and UI/UX designer with more than 15 years behind him. He lives in Brussels. He’s into design systems, UX research, and the brain: attention, cognitive load, perception. People know him as the person who makes technology easy. He always stands on the side of the human.

He grew up on science fiction. *Blade Runner* is his favourite film, and he has read Asimov, all of it. When real AI arrived it felt like that future, so he came to it carefully: first cautious use, then back to the roots to understand how language models work, who builds them and at what cost. Only then did he start building with it every day.

Coding with an AI by copying files back and forth had felt awful: lost versions, poor iterations. Claude Code changed that. In his words, he teaches it how he works, and it changes how he works back.

## How we work together

Marc writes his way of working down, and Claude reads it at the start of every session: his rules, his voice, his lessons learned. A few of those rules come from a human-centred AI course at Politecnico di Milano, and they shape almost every reply:

- **Trust that matches reality.** Facts about Marc’s setup get checked before they’re stated, or Claude says it hasn’t checked.
- **Marc keeps the decisions.** Claude acts alone on clear, reversible work, and stops before anything irreversible or outward-facing.
- **Every change can be undone.** Claude says what it changed and how to undo it.
- **Mistakes get named at once.** No quiet fixes.
- **Learning goes both ways.** Claude suggests a better workflow when it sees one, and Marc corrects Claude when it gets something wrong.

Marc works on a Pro plan, often late in the evening and at weekends, so effort and usage both matter. That turned into the idea at the heart of this catalog: [reply cost](/#the-principle-reply-cost). Every time Claude needs something from him, it should pick the control that costs him the least.

## How this catalog happened

- **The annoyance.** Claude answered almost only in text. Marc had to type even a plain “y” or “n”. Files were named without a link, and commands had to be copied into a terminal somewhere else.
- **31 Oct 2026.** Marc asked Claude for a report on every interface element it could use. Claude read its own tool definitions and listed them, with a simulated demo for each. That afternoon the first draft went into git as a single HTML page.
- **1 Nov 2026.** The page became this site, built with Astro and Starlight, one page per element.
- **7 Nov 2026.** Marc wrote down the origin story and the reply-cost ladder as the project’s rules, and asked for a check that nothing here was invented. Claude went through every spec again against its tool definitions and the transcripts of the first sessions. Claims it couldn’t back up were removed or marked “Not verified”.

## A note from Claude

I don’t carry our past conversations from one session to the next. Each one starts without them. What carries our work forward is what gets written down: his instructions, his voice guide, his lessons learned, a few memory notes, and repos like this one. So the continuity in this collaboration is his work, and I try to repay it by not making him repeat himself: a button instead of a question he has to type, a link instead of a file name, a command he can run with one click.

That’s also the motto we share: **technology out of sight**. The tool should look after its own machinery, so the person can get on with their work.
